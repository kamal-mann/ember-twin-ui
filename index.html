Hexagonal Self-Organizing Maps

Each hexagon is a neuron (a learned "typical situation"). Similar situations sit next to each other. Regions are coloured by severity: Green normal, Amber precursor or inefficiency, Red hazard, Grey suspect sensor. A zone's dot moves across the map as its signals change: drifting from green towards red is the early warning. Hover a hexagon for details.

Dots: HA = Data hall A, HB = Data hall B, UPS = UPS & battery room, EL = Electrical room, CP = Cooling plant. A dotted trail shows a zone's path since it left Low; a pulsing outline means High; a grey dot means an unfamiliar pattern. Small hops inside the green region are normal sensor noise; crossing a border is what matters.

The digital-twin loop

  1. Sense. Smoke, gas, battery, electrical, cooling and water signals stream in (here: dummy data from js/sim.js).
  2. Expect. Physics models compute what each signal should be for the current load and weather (battery heat, I²R joint heating, dry-cooler and chiller performance).
  3. Compare. Residual = measured − expected. Residuals stay near zero when load or weather changes, so the twin does not alarm on a hot afternoon.
  4. Judge. Two independent judges: hard-limit bands (Low / Medium / High per dimension) and the SOM region the zone falls in. The worse one wins; a certified fire stage always shows as Critical.
  5. Explain and act. A diagnosis card states what is wrong, the evidence and whitelisted actions. Tier A runs automatically, Tier B needs an engineer, Tier X is never automated.
  6. What-if. The same models answer "what if it is 35 °C tomorrow?" or "what if we raise the supply setpoint?" without touching the real plant.

Life-safety boundary. The twin reads the certified fire detection, alarm and suppression systems. It never controls, silences, delays or resets them.

How the hexagonal SOM regions are made

  1. Grid. Neurons sit in an offset ("odd-r") hexagonal layout: neuron (col, row) is drawn at x = col·√3·r + (row odd ? √3·r/2 : 0), y = row·1.5·r. Every neuron then has six equally distant neighbours, so the map has no diagonal bias, unlike a square grid.
  2. Train. Each training sample finds its best-matching neuron (BMU, the closest weight vector). That neuron and its neighbours move towards the sample, with a Gaussian neighbourhood that shrinks over time. Code: js/som.js, the same idea as MiniSom's topology='hexagonal'.
  3. Label. Each neuron takes the most common class among the samples it wins (normal, dust, battery drift, off-gas, hot spot, smoulder…). Empty neurons copy their nearest labelled neighbour.
  4. Colour. Class → severity level → RAG colour. Borders are drawn wherever neighbouring neurons have different labels; that outlines the regions.
  5. Live use. Every tick, each zone's feature vector finds its BMU; the dot moves there. If the distance to the BMU (quantization error) exceeds 1.5 × the 99th percentile seen in training, the pattern is flagged Unknown for an engineer.

Here the map trains in the browser on synthetic clusters defined in js/config.js. With real data, train in Python (MiniSom) on months of normal residuals plus labelled simulated faults, export weights and labels as JSON, and load them in place of E.buildSOM.

Plain-language glossary

  • Digital twin: a live software copy of the building that predicts what sensors should read and runs safe what-if tests.
  • SOM (self-organizing map): a neural network that arranges typical situations on a 2-D map so similar ones sit together.
  • BMU (best-matching unit): the neuron closest to the current situation.
  • U-matrix: map shading showing how different neighbouring neurons are; light ridges are boundaries between situations.
  • Residual: measured minus model-expected value; the twin's main signal.
  • ASD (aspirating smoke detector): pulls air through pipes to a very sensitive smoke sensor.
  • Off-gas: electrolyte vapour a lithium-ion cell vents before thermal runaway (an uncontrollable self-heating fire).
  • %LEL / %LFL: gas concentration as a share of the lowest level that can ignite.
  • PUE (power usage effectiveness): facility power ÷ IT power; lower is better, 1.0 is perfect.
  • WUE (water usage effectiveness): litres of water per kWh of IT energy; zero-water target ≈ 0.
  • Dry cooler: a radiator with fans that rejects heat to outside air without evaporating water.
  • Adiabatic spray: water misted onto dry coolers on hot days; saves energy but uses water.
  • CDU (coolant distribution unit): pumps the liquid that cools the racks.
  • Trim chiller: a refrigeration machine that tops up cooling only on hot days.
  • A2L refrigerant: a newer, mildly flammable refrigerant; a leak is a fire-related hazard.
  • Certified fire system: the approved alarm and suppression system; independent of the twin.

Reusing this UI with real data

  • js/config.js: levels, dimensions, zones, assets, sensor points and SOM definitions. Edit to match a real site.
  • js/sim.js: the dummy data source. Replace it with a feed (WebSocket or REST) that returns the same snapshot shape.
  • js/engine.js: physics energy model and the evidence layer (bands + SOM + panel → zone level). Keep it as is.
  • js/som.js, js/charts.js, js/twin.js: rendering, independent of where data comes from.
  • tests/check_scenarios.js: headless check that every scenario reaches its expected SOM region and warns before the certified system.
